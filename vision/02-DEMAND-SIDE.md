# The demand side: output needs someone able to choose it

Producing more is different from creating a sustainable exchange. An AI system might generate excellent work while the intended customer lacks money, time, trust or permission to use it. As production becomes easier, the organisation of demand deserves as much attention as the organisation of supply.

## Four questions that output does not answer

Who benefits from the result? Who can pay? Who chooses the supplier? Who bears the cost if it fails? These roles often belong to different people. A patient may benefit while an insurer purchases and a clinician remains accountable. A public service may be funded collectively while its users have little influence over procurement.

The distinction matters when evaluating apparent adoption. Supplier revenue can demonstrate purchases without establishing durable customer benefit. Repeated use can reflect usefulness, a subsidy, integration costs or dependence. Better evidence connects the purchased capability to an outcome, includes total costs and examines an appropriate comparison.

## Three conditional paths

In one path, AI complements workers and their higher incomes support new demand. In another, output grows while income and ownership concentrate, making access to necessities depend more heavily on public provision or transfers. In a third, practical productivity disappoints and ordinary budget constraints remain dominant. These are mechanisms to investigate, not an exhaustive forecast distribution.

Public institutions may become important buyers because some benefits cannot be captured by individual customers. But an institution's purchasing power does not establish that its purchases represent public priorities. Procurement needs scrutiny, contestable objectives and effective remedies alongside technical expertise.

## Demand is also an interface to power

Intermediaries can decide which products a customer sees or which needs become economically legible. Concentration in AI infrastructure raises one set of concerns; concentration in the interfaces that select and purchase services raises another. The OECD documents structural competition issues in the infrastructure supply chain. Our extension to demand-facing intermediaries is a hypothesis requiring its own evidence. [OECD infrastructure study, 2025](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/competition-in-artificial-intelligence-infrastructure_623d1874-en.html).

The practical implication is to study real alternatives. Can a person change providers, question a recommendation or organise with others to purchase differently? Lower production costs can improve those options, but do not supply them automatically.
