# The monolatry frame: a metaphor for asymmetric dependence

Imagine a community relying on a powerful provider it cannot fully inspect, replace or compel. The language of covenant, interpretation and obligation can illuminate that dependence. This chapter uses religious imagery as an analytical metaphor. It does not claim that historical religions are equivalent to AI platforms or that a theological analogy predicts technological behaviour.

## Four mechanisms to investigate

**Changing obligations.** A provider can alter the conditions of access after people have organised their lives around a service. The relevant evidence is the change in terms and its practical effects, rather than whether users initially accepted an agreement.

**Self-sealing explanations.** A community might explain every failure as proof that it needs to comply more closely. The test is whether there is a possible observation that would count against the provider's claims. Criticism becomes difficult when adverse evidence is always absorbed as a user's fault.

**Bounded belonging.** Benefits may be attached to membership rather than humanity in general. An arrangement can be generous internally while excluding outsiders. Investigate admission, expulsion, voice and the treatment of people who decline membership.

**Interpretive power.** When a system is difficult to understand, people who translate its outputs can acquire authority. They may improve comprehension or shield decisions from scrutiny. The difference depends on whether explanations can be challenged and checked independently.

## Where the metaphor fails

An actual technology has organisations, contracts, infrastructure and identifiable decisions behind it. Treating it as an inscrutable higher being can conceal those ordinary points of intervention. Opacity may come from technical difficulty, withheld information or institutional incentives; those causes require different responses.

The normative alternative is not that every citizen must understand every model parameter. People need workable representation, independent expertise, public reasons and remedies. UDHR Article 21 supplies one public reference for participation in government; it does not prescribe a particular AI governance design. ## A useful limit

Use the metaphor only where it helps identify a testable relationship. If it turns every capable system into a deity and every dissatisfied user into a believer, it stops discriminating between institutions.

OMNI-102 and OMNI-104 are conceptual contexts for the dependence mechanisms. They do not establish the historical analogy or assign probabilities. This chapter has no executable assurance.
