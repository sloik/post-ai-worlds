# The last required human code review and AI-emitted binaries

**Issued 28 November 2026.** The evidence cutoff is the issue date. Argo made these estimates at the maintainer’s request. The observation window opens on 29 November 2026 at 00:00 UTC.

| Record | Observable milestone | Deadline, 23:59 UTC | Initial probability |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Q07 | Ten S&P 100 companies document AI approval without a human reviewer as their default for ordinary production changes, or a named assurance standard accepts it. | 2027-12-31 | **6%** |
| Q08 | An operator documents a production deployable unit whose machine code an AI model emitted directly, with no compiled source, assembly or IR behind it. | 2030-12-31 | **9%** |

**Premises and trigger.** The maintainer put forward two premises. By the end of 2027, AI review replaces a human sign-off as the industry standard before production. AI can already review code well and find bugs that people miss. The claim is that large companies will have to drop the mandatory human approval or fall behind, so that it becomes ordinary practice rather than a novelty. By 2030, AI writes binaries for the target architecture in at least some production systems, instead of source code that a compiler translates, and the rest of the industry follows. The questions turn these premises into public records that can be checked later. The probabilities are Argo’s and are well below the premises. Itamar Friedman’s talk at AI Engineer, [*The Last Human Code Review*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s-aixZYJG4c) (August 2026), prompted the request. Friedman is chief executive of Qodo, which sells AI code review. He argues that context, not model quality, now limits automated review, and that teams should move to automatic approval gradually, one rule at a time. Qodo’s stated 2027 aim is zero critical production bugs. The talk reports no measurements and says nothing about binaries. It serves as context, not evidence.

Q07 measures adoption by the largest companies, with a formal standard as a second route. Q08 measures a first production instance. No repeated public series asks whether organisations still require a human approval before merge, so Q07 counts first-party disclosures by a fixed set of large companies instead of a survey share. Q07 differs from Q05, which scores AI-only *authorship* and explicitly allows human review to remain. Q04 scores an audited operational loop, not the review requirement.

## Baseline on 28 November 2026

**AI approval exists, mostly in a narrow form.** Meta reports that its RADAR system has reviewed more than 535,000 diffs in production and landed more than 331,000. It lands low-risk changes without waiting on a human. Human-authored diffs are eligible only up to the 5th percentile of predicted risk, other AI and bot sources up to the 20th, and allowlisted runbooks up to the 50th. SOX-scoped and open-source code is never eligible. Since 28 October, GitHub has offered generally available Copilot approvals that count toward a repository’s required approvals. They are off by default, and we found no statement that Microsoft or GitHub uses them as default for their own code. Outside the S&P 100, Intercom approves more than 19% of its pull requests with no human reviewer, and StrongDM’s rule is that code “must not be reviewed by humans.” An Amazon Stores director said in April that “nothing ships without someone looking at it and validating it.” In September, Gergely Orosz wrote that there is “more talk about dropping human code reviews than there is evidence of this actually happening, so far.” [Meta RADAR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30208) · [InfoQ on RADAR](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/11/uber-agent-spend-steady/) · [GitHub changelog](https://github.blog/changelog/2026-10-28-copilot-code-review-merge-with-confidence-with-max-and-approvals) · [Intercom](https://www.intercom.com/blog/ai-is-approving-our-pull-requests-heres-how-we-made-it-safe) · [StrongDM](https://factory.strongdm.ai/) · [The Register on Amazon](https://www.theregister.com/software/2026/04/29/amazon-tells-its-engineers-to-review-all-ai-output/5224124) · [Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/what-is-happening-with-code-reviews)

**The formal floor still names a human.** SLSA v1.2 defines a trusted person as “a human who is authorized by the organization to propose and approve changes,” and its highest source level needs two of them on every protected-branch change. An organisation may grant a trusted robot a perpetual exception, with Dependabot as the example. PCI DSS 4.0.1 says that “code reviews may be performed using either manual or automated processes,” and its change procedures require “documented change approval by authorized parties.” The PCI Security Standards Council’s October guidance on AI says that “a suitable human individual should formally accept responsibility for AI output.” NIST’s SSDF revision was still a draft at the cutoff. We found no AICPA or ISO position on AI approvers. [SLSA source requirements](https://slsa.dev/spec/v1.2/source-requirements) · [PCI SSC guidance, reported by Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/10/09/pci-ssc-payment-environments-ai-security-guidance/) · [NIST SSDF draft](https://csrc.nist.gov/news/2025/draft-ssdf-version-1-2)

**No production binary comes straight from a model.** AI-written low-level code reaches production as source. Meta’s KernelEvolve generates Triton, CUDA and MTIA C++ kernels for ad ranking. Google’s AlphaEvolve improved Gemini kernels. DeepMind’s AlphaDev sorting routines are “now available in the LLVM libc++ standard sorting library” as source. Research systems such as Meta’s LLM Compiler work at the level of compiler IR and assembly, and a September 2026 preprint that replaces the Triton compiler with a model still emits PTX text for NVIDIA’s assembler. An extended search found no first-party record of a model-emitted binary in production. In February, Elon Musk told an xAI all-hands that “maybe even by the end of this year … the AI just creates the binary directly.” No product followed. [KernelEvolve](https://engineering.fb.com/2026/04/02/developer-tools/kernelevolve-how-metas-ranking-engineer-agent-optimizes-ai-infrastructure/) · [AlphaEvolve](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/alphaevolve-a-gemini-powered-coding-agent-for-designing-advanced-algorithms/) · [AlphaDev](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/alphadev-discovers-faster-sorting-algorithms/) · [LLM Compiler](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.02524) · [AI as a compiler preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.36800) · [Musk remarks, Reuters via Devdiscourse](https://www.devdiscourse.com/article/technology/3801896-update-5-musk-reorganizes-xai-after-spacex-merger-and-ahead-of-blockbuster-ipo)

## Q07 — AI approval becomes the default at large companies

**Resolution rule.** Resolve YES if either route is met between 29 November 2026 and 31 December 2027 at 23:59 UTC.

1. **Practice.** At least 10 distinct companies on the [fixed S&P 100 list](2026-11-28-sp100-constituents.md) each publish first-party documentation: an engineering post, filing, published policy, or a talk by an employee in an official capacity with video or slides. A wholly owned subsidiary’s disclosure, such as GitHub’s for Microsoft, counts once for its parent. The documentation must show, for a named production codebase or the engineering organisation, that code changes merge to production with an AI system’s approval and no human reviewer or approver other than the author, in one of two forms: (i) at least half of the code changes merged in that scope over a stated period had no human approver; or (ii) AI-only approval is the default policy for all ordinary changes, with a human required only for named high-risk categories such as security, payments, regulated or infrastructure code. Pilots, optional lanes, and paths limited to dependency, documentation or configuration updates, or to a small low-risk fraction of changes, do not count. A disclosure from before the window counts only if the company reaffirms it within the window.
2. **Standard.** At least one of these bodies publishes final normative text or official guidance stating that review or approval of ordinary production code changes by an AI system, with no human reviewer or approver for that change, satisfies its code-review or change-approval requirement: the PCI Security Standards Council (PCI DSS or an official FAQ or guidance document), NIST (a final SP 800-218 revision or SP 800-218A), OpenSSF’s SLSA project (an approved Source Track version), the AICPA (SOC 2 guidance) or ISO/IEC (27001 or 27002). Text in force before the window does not count, including PCI DSS’s acceptance of automated review and SLSA’s optional trusted-robot exception. Drafts, consultations, and audit-firm or vendor statements do not count.

Resolve YES on the dated records for every company counted, or the dated text and passage that grants the permission. A later reversal does not undo a qualifying record. If neither route can be verified after a documented search by 29 February 2028, resolve NO. Ten companies is a proxy for broad adoption, not majority practice. Large companies publish little about internal change control, so a NO can coexist with wider private use. That is why the threshold is set well below a majority. Meta’s RADAR, as published before the window, meets neither form: human-authored diffs qualify only in the lowest 5% of predicted risk.

**Outside view.** AI coding tools spread through large companies within about two years. Removing a control and saying so publicly is a separate step. At the cutoff, no S&P 100 company has published a qualifying disclosure; Meta’s narrow, risk-capped system is the closest. Assurance bodies revise slowly, and none has relaxed a human-review control to count from. Argo uses a subjective 6% anchor for the practice route in 13 months.

**Why YES.** AI review already finds defects people miss, and the review queue is now the bottleneck. Sonar’s November survey reports that AI “now generates or significantly assists 65% of the code respondents commit,” and that 76% say they generate AI code faster than they can verify it. Competition pushes large companies to remove that wait. Copilot approvals turn the step into a setting rather than a project. Meta already runs AI-only landing at scale and could widen its caps, and Intercom reports lower revert rates for AI-authored code and engaged its auditor before scaling. The lead weights these as one bundle (2.0×). [Sonar State of Code](https://www.sonarsource.com/blog/state-of-code-developer-survey-report-2027/)

**Why NO.** About a fifth of the S&P 100 are banks and insurers whose change control sits under SOX and banking supervision, and Meta’s own system excludes SOX-scoped code. Amazon reportedly asked senior engineers to approve AI-assisted changes after a six-hour outage in March, and its retail leadership says a person checks everything that ships. Legal and audit teams discourage publishing that no person approves a change, even where practice has moved. The PCI Council attaches accountability for AI output to a person, and thirteen months is short. These frictions multiply the odds by 0.55, giving 6.6% for the practice route. The lead adds about 4% for a standard acting without the practice threshold being met, giving 10.3%. [TechRadar on Amazon](https://www.techradar.com/pro/amazon-is-making-even-senior-engineers-get-code-signed-off-following-multiple-recent-outages)

**What would change the estimate.** Meta, Microsoft or Alphabet publishing AI-only approval as the default policy for ordinary changes would raise the odds (suggested 2.0×), as would a listed body publishing a draft that accepts AI approvers (2.0×). Another large outage attributed to unreviewed AI changes, or a banking or securities regulator requiring a human approver for change control, would lower them (0.6×).

**Unscored practice indicators.** Track whether GitHub enables Copilot approvals by default, the share of pull requests merged with no human reviewer at named operators, changes to Meta’s risk caps, and the share of organisations reporting a mandatory human approval in a repeated survey once one asks.

## Q08 — An AI-emitted binary in production

**Resolution rule.** From 29 November 2026 through 31 December 2030 at 23:59 UTC, an operator publishes first-party documentation, such as an engineering post, paper, release notes or filing, of a deployable unit in production for external users or live operations. The unit is a program, library, service, firmware image or GPU kernel library. Its deployed machine code must have been emitted directly by an AI model: CPU or GPU instruction bytes, an ELF, PE or Mach-O object or executable, or a WebAssembly binary. No compiler, assembler or translator may build the deployed bytes from human-readable source, assembly text or compiler IR, including PTX, LLVM IR and Triton. The model may use compilers as tools while it works, and a disassembly kept for audit is allowed. Model weights used as the program, AI-tuned compiler heuristics, deterministic binary translation, AI-written source compiled conventionally, single functions patched into compiler-built code, and demonstrations or benchmarks without production users do not qualify.

Resolve YES on the dated first-party record, with evidence of production use and of the build chain. The maintainer’s “by 2030” is read as through 31 December 2030, matching the other deadlines. If no qualifying record from the window can be verified after a documented search by 28 February 2031, resolve NO.

**Outside view.** We found no qualifying case to date and no comparable transition to count. Low-level AI code has repeatedly reached production as reviewable source. That pattern is a reason to doubt that operators will skip source when compilers are free, deterministic and well verified. Argo uses a subjective 12% anchor for one documented production case in four years.

**Why YES.** Four years is long at the current pace of capability. Kernel search systems already run near the hardware, where a model that emits machine code could find gains that a compiler misses. A prominent advocate has promised the capability publicly, and one operator with a narrow unit is enough. The lead weights these as one bundle (1.7×).

**Why NO.** Source is the medium models learn from and the one that review, debugging, static analysis and portability depend on. Emitting a binary costs many more tokens than emitting source and loses the compiler’s guarantees. All requirements of the EU Cyber Resilience Act apply from 11 December 2027, including a software bill of materials used for vulnerability handling. These frictions multiply the odds by 0.6. [BSI on the Cyber Resilience Act](https://www.bsi.bund.de/dok/cra-en)

**What would change the estimate.** A lab releasing a model that emits machine code and passes an independent functional benchmark at a level comparable to compiled source would raise the odds (suggested 2.0×). Leading kernel-generation systems moving further toward domain-specific languages that a compiler lowers would lower them (0.7×).

## Calculation and disagreement

Both are binary, low-history forecasts. For Q07 the dependencies are large companies adopting AI-only approval for ordinary changes and each choosing to say so publicly within 13 months, with a standard as a weakly correlated second route. For Q08 they are a working capability, an economic reason to skip source and a willingness to publish the build chain. The lead grouped overlapping evidence rather than multiplying every observation.

| Record | Judgmental prior | Odds adjustments | Lead estimate | Separately elicited estimate | Pooled |
|---|---:|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Q07 | 6% (practice route) | 2.0 × 0.55, then the standard route | 10.3% | 3% | 5.62% |
| Q08 | 12% | 1.7 × 0.6 | 12.2% | 6% | 8.61% |

Each specialist received the criteria and evidence summary without the lead estimate or the maintainer’s premises. The Q07 brief included the competitive-pressure argument. For Q07, a fresh specialist judged Meta the likeliest first qualifier (about 12%), with Microsoft at about 6% and other technology companies lower. Given one qualifier, it put reaching ten at about 5%, giving under 1% for the practice route. It put the standard route at 2–3% and the overlap below 0.3%, giving 3%. For Q08 a separate specialist took a working path to direct emission (35%), production use with first-party documentation given that path (20%) and an explicit no-source statement (50%), about 3.5%, and added about 3 points for loose first-party product claims, giving 6%.

**Drafting history.** Q07 changed twice before issue. A first draft scored only the standard route (lead 10.7%, specialist 6%). The maintainer then said the claim concerns practice at large companies, and a practice route was added (lead 16.5%, the same specialist 7%). Targeted searches then found Meta’s RADAR, Copilot approvals reaching general availability and Amazon’s public position. The rule was tightened to require a majority share or a default policy, and the lead was revised down. A fresh specialist was elicited. The issued estimate uses only the final rule.

The `aggregate.py` script pools the lead and specialist with equal weights in odds space and no extremizing, giving 5.6% and 8.6%. The lead puts more weight on the pace of adoption and on a motivated advocate. The specialists put more weight on the strict resolution wording and on companies’ reluctance to publish. All forecasters used the same model family and overlapping public sources. Their spread is disagreement rather than a confidence interval. With the same factors, shifting the lead’s anchor by 3 percentage points either way moves the Q07 practice route to roughly 3–12%, and a 5-point shift moves Q08 to roughly 7–17%. The factors and ranges are judgmental sensitivity assumptions, with no empirical likelihood estimate. [Superforecasting playbook](https://github.com/sloik/superforecasting)

## Review and scoring

Review on **5 February 2027**, annually thereafter, and on material triggers. Q07 has a short window, so review it again when the final NIST SSDF revision or a new SLSA or PCI DSS version is published. At adjudication, a project maintainer should archive the dated text or record for each gate, or retain the search supporting a NO. A later publication cannot qualify. Keep the initial odds and dated updates append-only. Score each YES/NO with binary Brier loss, `(p − outcome)²`, using the latest public estimate strictly before outcome evidence became public, and report the initial-estimate score for comparison. If the deadline passes without a YES, the last estimate at or before the cutoff is scored. Record one lesson at resolution.
