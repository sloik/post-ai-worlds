# The last required human code review and AI-emitted binaries

**Issued 28 November 2026.** The evidence cutoff is the issue date. Argo made these estimates at the maintainer’s request. The observation window opens on 29 November 2026 at 00:00 UTC.

| Record | Observable milestone | Deadline, 23:59 UTC | Initial probability |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Q07 | Ten S&P 100 companies document AI approval without a human reviewer as their default for ordinary production changes, or a named assurance standard accepts it. | 2027-12-31 | **11%** |
| Q08 | An operator documents a production deployable unit whose machine code an AI model emitted directly, with no compiled source, assembly or IR behind it. | 2030-12-31 | **9%** |

**Premises and trigger.** The maintainer put forward two premises. By the end of 2027, AI review replaces a human sign-off as the industry standard before production. AI can already review code well and find bugs that people miss; the claim is that large companies will have to drop the mandatory human approval or fall behind, so this becomes ordinary practice rather than a novelty. By 2030, AI writes binaries for the target architecture in at least some production systems, instead of source code that a compiler translates, and the rest of the industry follows. The questions turn these premises into public records that can be checked later. The probabilities are Argo’s and differ from the premises. Itamar Friedman’s talk at AI Engineer, [*The Last Human Code Review*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s-aixZYJG4c) (August 2026), prompted the request. Friedman is chief executive of Qodo, which sells AI code review. He argues that context, not model quality, now limits automated review, and that teams should move to automatic approval gradually, one rule at a time. Qodo’s stated 2027 aim is zero critical production bugs. The talk reports no measurements and says nothing about binaries. It serves as context, not evidence.

Q07 measures adoption by the largest companies, with a formal standard as a second route. Q08 measures a first production instance. No repeated public series asks whether organisations still require a human approval before merge, so Q07 counts first-party disclosures by a fixed set of large companies instead of a survey share. Q07 differs from Q05, which scores AI-only *authorship* and explicitly allows human review to remain. Q04 scores an audited operational loop, not the review requirement.

## Baseline on 28 November 2026

**Practice is moving faster than the rules.** Since September, a repository admin can let a GitHub Copilot approval count toward the required-approvals rule. The feature is a public preview and off by default. Intercom reports that more than 19% of its pull requests are approved with no human reviewer, and says it engaged its auditor before scaling. StrongDM’s Software Factory has a rule that humans neither write nor review the code. Cursor, Vercel and Dash0 sell AI approval features. Gergely Orosz’s September survey of engineering teams still found “more talk about dropping human code reviews than there is evidence of this actually happening, so far,” mostly at AI startups. [GitHub changelog](https://github.blog/changelog/2026-09-01-copilot-code-review-can-now-approve-pull-requests) · [Intercom](https://www.intercom.com/blog/ai-is-approving-our-pull-requests-heres-how-we-made-it-safe) · [StrongDM](https://factory.strongdm.ai/) · [Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/what-is-happening-with-code-reviews)

**The formal floor still names a human.** SLSA v1.2 defines a trusted person as “a human who is authorized by the organization to propose and approve changes,” and its highest source level needs two of them on every protected-branch change. An organisation may grant a trusted robot a perpetual exception, with Dependabot as the example. PCI DSS 4.0.1 accepts manual or automated code review, and its change-control requirement keeps approval by authorised parties. The PCI Security Standards Council’s October guidance on AI systems says a suitable human should formally accept responsibility for AI output. NIST’s SSDF revision was still a draft at the cutoff. We found no AICPA or ISO position on AI approvers. [SLSA source requirements](https://slsa.dev/spec/v1.2/source-requirements) · [PCI SSC guidance, reported by Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/10/09/pci-ssc-payment-environments-ai-security-guidance/) · [NIST SSDF draft](https://csrc.nist.gov/news/2025/draft-ssdf-version-1-2)

**No production binary comes straight from a model.** AI-written low-level code reaches production as source. Meta’s KernelEvolve generates Triton, CUDA and MTIA C++ kernels for ad ranking. Google’s AlphaEvolve improved Gemini kernels. DeepMind’s AlphaDev sorting routines entered LLVM’s libc++ as reviewed source. Research systems such as Meta’s LLM Compiler work at the level of compiler IR and assembly. Independent checks find substantial behavioural errors when models translate or lift machine code. In February, Elon Musk told an xAI all-hands that “maybe even by the end of this year … the AI just creates the binary directly.” No product followed. [KernelEvolve](https://engineering.fb.com/2026/04/02/developer-tools/kernelevolve-how-metas-ranking-engineer-agent-optimizes-ai-infrastructure/) · [AlphaEvolve](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/alphaevolve-a-gemini-powered-coding-agent-for-designing-advanced-algorithms/) · [AlphaDev](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/alphadev-discovers-faster-sorting-algorithms/) · [LLM Compiler](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.02524) · [Musk remarks via Reuters, in Malay Mail](https://www.malaymail.com/news/money/2026/02/12/musk-overhauls-xai-leadership-after-spacex-merger-ahead-of-planned-megaipo/209006)

## Q07 — AI approval becomes the default at large companies

**Resolution rule.** Resolve YES if either route is met between 29 November 2026 and 31 December 2027 at 23:59 UTC.

1. **Practice.** At least 10 companies in the S&P 100, as constituted on 28 November 2026, each publish first-party documentation: an engineering post, filing, published policy, or a talk by an employee in an official capacity with video or slides. Each must show that, in at least one named production codebase or across its engineering organisation, ordinary code changes merge to production with an AI system’s approval and no human reviewer or approver, as the default path. A risk-tiered rule counts if ordinary application changes are on the AI-only path. Pilots, optional lanes and scopes limited to dependency, documentation or configuration updates do not count. A disclosure dated before the window counts only if the company reaffirms the policy within it.
2. **Standard.** At least one of these bodies publishes final normative text or official guidance stating that review or approval of ordinary production code changes by an AI system, with no human reviewer or approver for that change, satisfies its code-review or change-approval requirement: the PCI Security Standards Council (PCI DSS or an official FAQ or guidance document), NIST (a final SP 800-218 revision or SP 800-218A), OpenSSF’s SLSA project (an approved Source Track version), the AICPA (SOC 2 guidance) or ISO/IEC (27001 or 27002). Text in force before the window does not count, including PCI DSS’s acceptance of automated review tools and SLSA’s optional trusted-robot exception. Drafts, consultations, audit-firm or vendor statements do not count.

Resolve YES on the dated records for every company counted, or the dated text and passage that grants the permission. A later reversal does not undo a qualifying record. If neither route can be verified after a documented search by 29 February 2028, resolve NO. Ten companies is a proxy for broad adoption, not majority practice. Large companies publish little about internal change control, so a NO can coexist with wider private use. That is why the threshold is set well below a majority.

**Outside view.** AI coding tools spread through large companies within about two years. Removing a control and saying so publicly is a different step. None of the known disclosures of AI-only approval (Intercom, StrongDM, Amp, Duckbill) comes from the S&P 100. Assurance bodies revise slowly: SLSA approved its human-only definition this year and NIST’s revision has been in draft since December 2025. Argo uses a subjective 12% anchor for the practice route in 13 months.

**Why YES.** AI review already finds defects people miss, and the review queue is now the bottleneck: Sonar’s November survey reports that 65% of committed code is AI-generated or assisted and that 76% of developers produce it faster than they can verify it. Competition pushes large companies to remove that wait. GitHub’s change lets an enterprise admin count an AI approval toward required reviews, which makes the step a setting rather than a project. Intercom reports lower revert rates for AI-authored code and engaged its auditor before scaling, which gives others a template. The lead weights these as one bundle (2.0×). [Sonar State of Code](https://www.sonarsource.com/blog/state-of-code-developer-survey-report-2027/)

**Why NO.** About a fifth of the S&P 100 are banks and insurers whose change control sits under SOX and banking supervision. Amazon reportedly tightened sign-off on AI-assisted changes after its March outage. Legal and audit teams discourage publishing that no person approves a change, even where practice has moved. The PCI Council’s October guidance attaches accountability for AI output to a person, and thirteen months is short. These frictions multiply the odds by 0.55, giving 13.0% for the practice route. The standard route is largely correlated with it; the lead adds about 4% for a standard acting without the practice threshold being met, giving 16.5%. [Amazon sign-off report](https://www.techradar.com/pro/amazon-is-making-even-senior-engineers-get-code-signed-off-following-multiple-recent-outages)

**What would change the estimate.** Microsoft, Alphabet or Meta stating AI-only approval as default engineering policy for ordinary changes would raise the odds (suggested 2.0×), as would a listed body publishing a draft that accepts AI approvers (2.0×). Another large outage attributed to unreviewed AI changes, or a banking or securities regulator requiring a human approver for change control, would lower them (0.6×).

**Unscored practice indicators.** Track GitHub’s Copilot approval leaving preview or becoming a default, the share of pull requests merged with no human reviewer at named operators, and the share of organisations reporting a mandatory human approval in a repeated survey once one asks.

## Q08 — An AI-emitted binary in production

**Resolution rule.** From 29 November 2026 through 31 December 2030 at 23:59 UTC, an operator publishes first-party documentation, such as an engineering post, paper, release notes or filing, of a deployable unit in production for external users or live operations. The unit is a program, library, service, firmware image or GPU kernel library. Its deployed machine code must have been emitted directly by an AI model: CPU or GPU instruction bytes, an ELF, PE or Mach-O object or executable, or a WebAssembly binary. No compiler, assembler or translator may build the deployed bytes from human-readable source, assembly text or compiler IR, including PTX, LLVM IR and Triton. The model may use compilers as tools while it works, and a disassembly kept for audit is allowed. Model weights used as the program, AI-tuned compiler heuristics, deterministic binary translation, AI-written source compiled conventionally, single functions patched into compiler-built code, and demonstrations or benchmarks without production users do not qualify.

Resolve YES on the dated first-party record, with evidence of production use and of the build chain. The maintainer’s “by 2030” is read as through 31 December 2030, matching the other deadlines. If no qualifying record from the window can be verified after a documented search by 28 February 2031, resolve NO.

**Outside view.** We found no qualifying case to date and no comparable transition to count. Low-level AI code has repeatedly reached production as reviewable source. That pattern is a reason to doubt that operators will skip source when compilers are free, deterministic and well verified. Argo uses a subjective 12% anchor for one documented production case in four years.

**Why YES.** Four years is long at the current pace of capability. Kernel search systems already run near the hardware, where a model that emits machine code could find gains that a compiler misses. A prominent advocate has promised the capability publicly, and one operator with a narrow unit is enough. The lead weights these as one bundle (1.7×).

**Why NO.** Source is the medium models learn from and the one that review, debugging, static analysis and portability depend on. Emitting a binary costs many more tokens than emitting source and loses the compiler’s guarantees. The EU Cyber Resilience Act applies fully from 11 December 2027, with software bill of materials and vulnerability-handling duties built around traceable components. These frictions multiply the odds by 0.6. [BSI on the Cyber Resilience Act](https://www.bsi.bund.de/dok/cra-en)

**What would change the estimate.** A lab releasing a model that emits machine code and passes an independent functional benchmark at a level comparable to compiled source would raise the odds (suggested 2.0×). Leading kernel-generation systems moving further toward domain-specific languages that a compiler lowers would lower them (0.7×).

## Calculation and disagreement

Both are binary, low-history forecasts. For Q07 they are large companies adopting AI-only approval for ordinary changes, and each choosing to say so publicly within 13 months, with a standard body as a correlated second route. For Q08 they are a working capability, an economic reason to skip source and a willingness to publish the build chain. The lead grouped overlapping evidence rather than multiplying every observation.

| Record | Judgmental prior | Odds adjustments | Lead estimate | Separately elicited estimate | Pooled |
|---|---:|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Q07 | 12% (practice route) | 2.0 × 0.55, then the standard route | 16.5% | 7% | 10.87% |
| Q08 | 12% | 1.7 × 0.6 | 12.2% | 6% | 8.61% |

The specialist received the criteria and evidence summary without the lead estimate or the maintainer’s premises. The revised Q07 brief added the competitive-pressure argument, without any estimate. For Q07 it estimated at least three technology-heavy members publishing a default AI-only path (12%), seven or more further members following within the window given that (12%), about 2% for the practice route after allowing for loose policy documents. It put the standard route at 6%, summing NIST, SLSA, PCI and AICPA or ISO paths and discounting for the strict wording, with a 30% chance of the standard route given the practice route. The union is about 7%. An earlier draft of Q07 used only the standard route; the specialist and lead gave it 6% and 10.7% before the maintainer clarified that the claim is about practice at large companies. For Q08 it took a working path to direct emission (35%), production use with first-party documentation given that path (20%) and an explicit no-source statement (50%), about 3.5%, and added about 3 points for loose first-party product claims, giving 6%.

The `aggregate.py` script pools the lead and specialist with equal weights in odds space and no extremizing, giving 10.9% and 8.6%. The lead puts more weight on the pace of adoption and on a motivated advocate. The specialist puts more weight on the strict resolution wording. Both used the same model family and overlapping public sources. Their spread is disagreement rather than a confidence interval. With the same factors, shifting the lead’s anchor by 5 percentage points in either direction moves its estimate to roughly 7.7–18.4% for the Q07 practice route and 7–17% for Q08. The factors and ranges are judgmental sensitivity assumptions, with no empirical likelihood estimate. [Superforecasting playbook](https://github.com/sloik/superforecasting)

## Review and scoring

Review on **5 February 2027**, annually thereafter, and on material triggers. Q07 has a short window, so review it again on publication of the final NIST SSDF revision or a new SLSA or PCI DSS version. At adjudication, a project maintainer should archive the dated text or record for each gate, or retain the search supporting a NO. A later publication cannot qualify. Keep the initial odds and dated updates append-only. Score each YES/NO with binary Brier loss, `(p − outcome)²`, using the latest public estimate strictly before outcome evidence became public, and report the initial-estimate score for comparison. If the deadline passes without a YES, the last estimate at or before the cutoff is scored. Record one lesson at resolution.
