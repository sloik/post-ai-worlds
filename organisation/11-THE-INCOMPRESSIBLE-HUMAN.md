# Answerability beyond a capability contest

The question behind this chapter is what remains important when machines perform more work competently. An appeal to a task humans happen to do better may be overtaken by technical progress. Responsibility is a different category: it concerns who owes what to whom, who bears consequences and what remedy is available.

The historical title should not be read as a claim of permanent human exclusivity. Omnicorp's proposed requirement is an answerable operator. Present arrangements ordinarily locate duties in people and recognised organisations; a future nonhuman arrangement remains a question requiring institutional design and evidence.

## Separate kinds of accountability

Operational accountability can include tracing a decision, finding an error and correcting a process. Legal liability concerns recognised duties and enforceable consequences. Moral responsibility raises further questions about agency and blame. Demonstrating the first does not settle the others.

Continuity may help an operator remain available when consequences arrive. Retaining model weights alone does not establish an acting identity, interests, assets or duties. Nor does a fluent explanation prove that the explanation faithfully reconstructs the process that produced a decision.

A useful assessment asks whether responsibility is identifiable, whether relevant records survive, whether sanctions or correction can have an effect, and whether an affected person can obtain a remedy. These are proposed questions rather than a sufficient philosophical definition.

## Recognition, provenance and responsibility

Being well known, being the original creator and being responsible for an outcome are different attributes. A product may benefit from all three, but demand for one should not be counted as evidence of another. A preference for human authorship can exist without demonstrating that a named author provides meaningful redress.

Answerability could become commercially valuable where buyers can verify it and use the available remedy. It can also be ignored or undermined. The project does not treat it as an indestructible market premium.

## Membership and meaningful participation

A person's standing should not depend solely on remaining economically indispensable. That is a normative position, not a prediction about organisational behaviour. If work is used to provide belonging, participants should know what it accomplishes and have a meaningful voice in its conditions.

Consent and practical exit are important safeguards. They are insufficient where deception, coercive dependence, external harms or exclusion from decisions remain. An internal role can be meaningful without being the only possible way to perform a task; delayed benefits and resilience also matter.

Founder-led groups can also confuse commitment to a purpose with agreement with their founder. Protected disagreement, independent review and rules that bind leaders are proposed safeguards. They require actual decision rights and consequences, rather than a ceremonial invitation to criticise.

Questions about possible AI welfare require separate consideration. Anthropic's constitution explicitly discusses uncertainty about AI experience and moral status; this documents a laboratory's position, not a scientific resolution. [Anthropic constitution](https://www.anthropic.com/constitution).

These distinctions apply across the [shared worlds](../worlds/README.md). Greater capability changes what an operator can do. It does not by itself determine what anyone is entitled to do to, or require of, that operator.
