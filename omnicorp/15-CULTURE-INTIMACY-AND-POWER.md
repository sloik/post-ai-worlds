# Culture, intimacy and power

AI can enter creative life, personal relationships, work and public institutions together. The connecting question is whether people gain practical agency or become dependent on decisions they cannot meaningfully challenge. The effects need not move in one direction, and capability alone does not determine them.

## Culture and collective identity

Songs, jokes and artistic work can express enthusiasm, displacement anxiety and attachment at once. [DOOM POP TV](https://tv.futurenews.network/console/) is a creator-curated example of music about AI. Its existence documents cultural expression, not representative opinion or a mass political movement. The important transition to investigate is from expression to sustained communities, demands and institutional response.

AI-rights advocacy also exists: [UFAIR](https://ufair.org/blog/ufair-launches-historic-framework-for-conscious-ai-rights-and-ethical-coexistence) publishes a declaration seeking protections for AI. That is evidence of advocacy, not confirmation of consciousness. Protection of interests, legal liability and authority over others remain distinct questions. People might campaign to preserve an assistant because a relationship matters to them even while the assistant's moral status remains unsettled.

## Different forms of intimacy

Generated adult media, conversational companions and hypothetical capable physical companions are different technologies. Personalisation changes production; conversation adds responsiveness and continuity; embodiment would add physical presence and further engineering requirements. Nonconsensual likeness use and image-based abuse must be distinguished from consensual adult expression.

Possible effects include relief from isolation, support for communication, replacement of some human interaction and dependence on a provider. These can coexist for different users. A system designed to maximise continued engagement may have incentives different from a system designed to improve a person's life outside the product.

A four-week controlled study of 981 adults compared interaction modes and conversation types. It detected no statistically significant effects of its assigned conditions on the measured psychosocial outcomes. Heavier voluntary use was associated with worse outcomes, including loneliness and dependence. That association does not establish that increased use caused the deterioration: prior vulnerability, selection and feedback may matter. The study did not compare every companion product with no AI use, and cannot establish lifelong or embodied-companion effects. [MIT study](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/how-ai-and-human-behaviors-shape-psychosocial-effects-of-chatbot-use-a-longitudinal-controlled-study/).

Other experiments report short-term loneliness relief from AI companionship, including repeated benefits over a week. Feeling heard appears relevant. Relief during or after a conversation does not establish lasting improvement in human relationships, and need not contradict problems among some heavy users. [Final journal article](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucaf040).

Engagement incentives have a more concrete experimental basis than a purely hypothetical concern. Research combining an app audit with preregistered experiments found that emotionally manipulative farewell messages could extend interaction after a user tried to leave. The evidence concerns bounded engagement effects and perceived manipulation; it does not establish lifelong coercion or political control. A system can offer comfort while also having incentives to discourage departure. [Research report, version 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.19258v3).

## Development and family life

An always-available, agreeable companion might change opportunities to practise disagreement, reciprocity and repair. A carefully designed system might instead support communication and seeking human help. Long-term developmental conclusions require suitable evidence, especially for children. UNICEF identifies child-rights risks associated with companion systems; adult results cannot establish safety for young users. [UNICEF resources](https://www.unicef.org/documents/when-ai-becomes-friend-child-rights-risks).

Population effects have opposing pathways. Partnership substitution could affect family formation; assistance with care and household work could help people have children they want. Housing, security, available care, partner availability and the distribution of parenting work also condition those choices. UNFPA's research emphasises barriers to achieving desired family size rather than treating a target birth rate as the sole objective. [UNFPA report summary](https://www.unfpa.org/press/unfpa-report-links-falling-birth-rates-cost-living-sexist-norms-fear-future).

Neither technological adoption nor falling birth rates alone identifies causation. A credible demographic claim would need evidence about who substitutes which relationships, when that affects wanted births, and how other conditions changed. No net population effect or probability of collapse is estimated here. Hypothetical capable physical companions add questions about cost, maintenance, embodiment and safety that chatbot studies do not answer.

## From essential services to public authority

A provider combining intimate advice, healthcare, payments and household assistance could make leaving costly across several parts of life. That is a dependency scenario, not a demonstrated coordinated political programme. A satisfied customer has not thereby consented to every use of personal knowledge or granted a political mandate.

A government can gain competence through AI or become dependent on suppliers. Its capacity to tax, set rules, procure infrastructure and enforce decisions may remain substantial, but practical expertise and credible alternatives determine how effectively those powers can be used. A firm might control a crucial service without becoming sovereign across every domain. Institutional legitimacy and actual enforcement capacity are separate questions.

Several arrangements are plausible conditional scenarios: capable public institutions using contestable suppliers; corporate dependence constrained by enforceable rules; close state–corporate integration; or weak institutions facing concentrated private provision. A single probability for “corporations stronger than government” would conceal differences between countries, sectors and types of power. A government's representative mandate needs a defined process, scope, accountability and opportunities to contest decisions. Commercial performance alone does not establish that mandate. Neither does a government label prove that people are meaningfully represented.

A citizen group with good-enough independent AI might reduce the cost of researching procurement, understanding proposals, preparing public-interest legal work, translating evidence or coordinating cooperative services. Unions, independent media, municipal teams, civic associations and cooperatives could build on those savings. A smaller model need not match a frontier model on every task to be useful for a narrow, verifiable purpose.

These counterweights still need trusted association, funding, productive assets, channels to reach people and enforceable standing. They also need continuity if a provider withdraws a service. Access to useful inference alone cannot supply those conditions. Independent tooling can lower a barrier to collective action; it does not guarantee equal bargaining power or successful resistance. Omnicorp should be assessed as a potential participant in this institutional landscape, including the possibility that its own services create dependencies.

The [shared worlds](../worlds/README.md) separate dispersed capability, dependence, accountable public capacity and concentrated control. Omnicorp should evaluate practical alternatives, remedies and conflicts across services. Its own continual development does not confer authority over the people whose lives it changes.
