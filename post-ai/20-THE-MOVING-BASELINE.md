# The moving baseline: measuring both sides of the comparison

When someone says AI has reached human level, which people, which tasks and which conditions are being compared? Neither side is a single constant. Models differ across versions and settings; people differ in expertise, resources, health and familiarity with the task. A meaningful comparison names those differences.

## Scores and the capacities they represent

A test score is an observation produced by an instrument. Interpreting change requires asking whether the instrument measures the same thing across groups and time. A decline in one series does not by itself establish a universal decline in intelligence, and a gain in another does not establish improvement in every practical capability.

Research on Norwegian cognitive-test cohorts finds patterns including a reversal within families and analyses environmental explanations. Its population and design matter. It is not evidence that every society is becoming less capable, that AI caused a decline or that literacy alone explains the pattern. [Bratsberg and Røgeberg, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1718793115).

## Assistance changes the unit of analysis

Should a model be compared with an unaided person, an experienced professional using tools, or a team? Each comparison can answer a different question. An assistant may raise a novice's performance while changing what expertise the organisation needs. A result for the combined system should not be attributed entirely to either component.

The comparison also depends on time and cost. A system producing a strong answer after extensive review is different from one handling a consequential task reliably without assistance. Both may be valuable, but their evidence should not be merged.

## Standing should not follow the scoreboard

People's right to be heard should not rise or fall with their relative test performance. At the same time, meaningful consent and participation can require support for comprehension. Institutions may need accessible explanations, independent advice and time to deliberate, rather than assuming a formal choice is always an informed one.

This is a normative commitment combined with an empirical research question. We should test whether particular supports help people understand and influence decisions; we should not treat a general claim of declining ability as a reason to remove their agency.
